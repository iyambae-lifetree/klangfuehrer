You don't need to replace a good old receiver or stereo system just because your music now lives on your phone. A small add-on box plugged into a free input on your amp is enough. That's either a Bluetooth receiver, which pairs directly with your phone, or a Wi-Fi streamer, which brings Spotify Connect, AirPlay 2 and the like onto your home network. What matters when you upgrade an old stereo

Bluetooth or Wi-Fi. A Bluetooth receiver is the simplest fix: pair your phone, hit play, done. But Bluetooth compresses the music for the wireless link, and how much depends on the codec, the method the sender and receiver use to package the data. Your phone also has to stay in range, and every phone call interrupts the music. A Wi-Fi streamer like the WiiM Pro pulls the music from the internet itself once you send it there via AirPlay 2, Chromecast or Spotify Connect in the Spotify app. Your phone just becomes the remote. We explain the difference in detail in our guide WiiM or Bluetooth receiver?.

Which phone you have. The high-quality Bluetooth codecs LDAC and aptX Adaptive only exist on Android phones, and not on every model. An iPhone sends audio over Bluetooth using AAC. If you're on an iPhone, the advantage of pricier Bluetooth receivers shrinks a lot, and AirPlay 2 over Wi-Fi is often the better choice.

Which inputs your amp has. Most older receivers and amps only have analog RCA inputs (labeled something like CD, Tuner, Tape or AUX). In that case, what counts is the quality of the add-on's built-in DAC, the digital-to-analog converter that turns the data back into an audio signal. If your amp has an optical (Toslink) or coaxial digital input, or you already have an external DAC on the shelf, you can hand the signal over digitally. Then the better device does the converting, and the add-on's analog output no longer matters.

Small things that shape daily use. Is a power adapter included? Does the range reach your couch? Do you need an app, or should the thing just work? The power adapter in particular is where the devices in this comparison differ.

WiiM Pro: why the pricier route often pays off

The WiiM Pro isn't a plain Bluetooth receiver. It's a network streamer that joins your home network over Wi-Fi or an Ethernet cable. It supports AirPlay 2 and Chromecast, and you can sign in to Spotify, Tidal, Qobuz, Amazon Music and Deezer directly. Several WiiM devices can play together if you want music in more than one room (multiroom). German site hifi.de gave it 7.8 out of 10, praising the features for the price and the app.

The biggest limitation is in the same hifi.de report: the analog RCA output is only adequate in sound quality. The converter inside (a TI PCM5121) is not the WiiM's strength. It really comes into its own when you connect it via its optical or coaxial output to an amp with a digital input or to an external DAC. Suitable converters with an optical input and RCA output, such as the FiiO K11 or the Topping DX1 II, are in our comparison of USB DACs under $200. Whether that extra step is worth it for you is covered in Is an external DAC worth it?.

Two more points. Over Bluetooth, the WiiM Pro only receives without high-resolution codecs, so Bluetooth here is more of a fallback for guests. And it has no phono preamp. A turntable on its RCA input still needs its own.

FiiO BR13: a lot of Bluetooth for little money

The BR13 brings LDAC and aptX Adaptive to a price range where that's rare. It has an RCA output (2 volts according to FiiO, the usual level of a CD player), plus optical and coaxial digital outputs up to 96 kHz and 24-bit. That puts the sample rate (how many times per second the signal is captured) and bit depth (how finely each value is resolved) above CD level. Over USB-C it also works as a small DAC for your computer, again up to 96 kHz. The app includes a 10-band equalizer.

Three things you should know. No power adapter is included, so you'll need your own USB-C charger. The high-quality codecs only help with compatible Android phones; with an iPhone it runs on AAC. And there are no independent measurements yet, as the measurement site audioreview.frieve.com also points out. At this price the risk is manageable, but our recommendation rests on features and the spec sheet, not on measurements.

FiiO BTA30 Pro: three devices in one box

The BTA30 Pro is a Bluetooth receiver, a Bluetooth transmitter and a USB DAC in one. As a receiver it accepts LDAC, aptX HD, aptX, AAC and SBC. As a transmitter it can, for example, send your TV's sound to Bluetooth headphones, even over LDAC, though not over AAC. In USB DAC mode an ESS ES9038Q2M chip handles PCM up to 32-bit/384 kHz and DSD256. That turns your old stereo into speakers for your computer at the same time. Thanks to the external antenna, retailer listings quote a range of up to 30 meters (about 100 ft).

The price for all that is a bit of a learning curve: you have to pick the operating mode deliberately, or you'll wonder why nothing is playing. One note on hookup: according to FiiO's spec sheet, the analog output is a pair of RCA jacks, so a regular RCA cable is all you need.

Audioengine B1 (Next Gen): check which version you get

Audioengine updated the B1 in early 2025: Bluetooth 5.3, aptX Adaptive and aptX HD, a new ESS converter, RCA and optical outputs, an aluminum case and a three-year warranty. LDAC is missing, so for Android users with an LDAC phone the BR13 is the cheaper option with more codecs.

The catch is at the retailer. When we checked the Amazon listing (on Amazon Germany), it was still running under the product number of the first generation from 2014. The title already listed the new specs, but one description text still mentioned an older converter. Which version actually ships was not certain, and at the time it was only available on backorder. US listings may differ, so check carefully: look for "Next Gen", Bluetooth 5.3 and aptX Adaptive on the box and on your receipt.

Which streamer or Bluetooth receiver is right for you?

  • You have an iPhone: Get the WiiM Pro. AirPlay 2 runs over Wi-Fi, and your phone couldn't use the fancy Bluetooth codecs of the other devices anyway.
  • You use Spotify, Tidal or Qobuz and want your stereo permanently on the network: Also the WiiM Pro, ideally connected digitally to an amp with a digital input or to a DAC.
  • You have an Android phone with LDAC or aptX Adaptive and just want to play music from it: The FiiO BR13 is enough. Budget for a USB-C power adapter.
  • Your stereo should also play from your computer, or your TV should send to Bluetooth headphones: The FiiO BTA30 Pro covers both in one device.
  • You don't want an app and value a long warranty: The Audioengine B1, if you get the new generation.
  • Your amp only has analog inputs and you want the best possible result: Run the WiiM Pro digitally into an external DAC and connect the DAC's RCA output to your amp. It costs more, but it bypasses the WiiM's weakest point.

And honestly: if you only play music from your phone now and then in the background, you don't need a streamer that costs close to $200. The BR13 is plenty.

What didn't make the list, and why

  • WiiM Mini: The cheaper little sibling of the Pro (hifi.de: 7.6 out of 10) was only available from third-party sellers when we checked Amazon Germany. It also lacks an Ethernet port, Chromecast and a power adapter.
  • WiiM Pro Plus and WiiM Amp: Both are above our $200 limit.
  • iFi Zen Blue V2: A very wide range of codecs and a balanced output, but it wasn't available at the German retailer we checked. Its successor, the Zen Blue 3, costs around $300, which is above our limit.
  • 1Mii B06HD+: LDAC and digital outputs at a low price, but no rating from the trade press, conflicting information about its Bluetooth version, a confusing number of model variants, and it was out of stock when we checked.
  • Arylic BR10: Cheap, but only a 3.5 mm output, USB DAC mode only up to 48 kHz, inconsistent manufacturer specs, and marked as discontinued by a major retailer.
  • Arylic S50 Pro+: A Wi-Fi preamp with lots of services, but above our $200 limit. Sound & Vision magazine also criticizes the app.
  • Pro-Ject BT Box E HD: A possible candidate for later. We haven't evaluated a trade-press rating yet, so it isn't listed here (yet).

FAQ

Which input on my old stereo should I plug the receiver into?

Any free line-level input such as AUX, CD, Tuner or Tape. Never the Phono input: that one is meant for turntables, adds a lot of gain and boosts the bass heavily. The result would be loud, boomy and distorted.

Does LDAC help if I have an iPhone?

No. iPhones send Bluetooth audio using AAC and don't support LDAC or aptX Adaptive. If you listen from an iPhone, a Wi-Fi streamer with AirPlay 2 like the WiiM Pro is usually the better investment.

Does a Wi-Fi streamer always sound better than Bluetooth?

Not automatically. Over Wi-Fi the music arrives without the extra Bluetooth compression. But what finally comes out of your speakers also depends on the converter. With the WiiM Pro, the analog output is the weak spot; connected digitally, it plays to its strengths.

Can I connect my TV to the stereo through the receiver?

For TV sound, an optical cable from the TV to a DAC or to an amp with a digital input is usually the more direct route. The FiiO BTA30 Pro can also send your TV's sound to Bluetooth headphones if you want to listen quietly at night.

Do these devices work with powered speakers too?

Yes, with any powered speaker that has an RCA or digital input. Which desk speakers work well for that is shown in our comparison of the best desktop speakers for music under $150.

Your old stereo stays what it always was; it just gets a new way in for the music.
