WiiM or Bluetooth Receiver: Which Is Right for Your Stereo?

WiiM or Bluetooth receiver for your stereo? When a Wi-Fi streamer is worth it, when Bluetooth is enough, and how iPhone, codecs and amp inputs decide.

A Bluetooth receiver is enough if you only want to send music from your phone to your stereo now and then, and you have an Android phone with good codecs. A Wi-Fi streamer like the WiiM Pro is worth it if you use an iPhone, listen to streaming services regularly, or want your stereo permanently on your home network. Three questions settle it: which phone, how often, and which inputs your amp has.

What's actually the difference?

With a Bluetooth receiver, your phone sends the music wirelessly straight to a small box connected to your amp. The phone stays the source: it has to stay in range, a phone call interrupts the music, and your battery is doing the work. The music gets compressed for the wireless link. How good that sounds depends on the codec, the method your phone and the receiver use to package the data. SBC is the basic standard, AAC is what the iPhone uses, and LDAC and aptX Adaptive are the more advanced options from the Android world.

With a Wi-Fi streamer, the device itself sits on your home network. When you send music to it via Chromecast or Spotify Connect in the Spotify app, it pulls the stream straight from the internet. Your phone just becomes the remote: you can take a call, leave the house or put the phone down, and the music keeps playing. With AirPlay 2, an iPhone sends audio over Wi-Fi instead of Bluetooth. On top of that you get things Bluetooth can't do, like playing music in several rooms at once (multiroom).

Why your phone is half the decision

The codecs Bluetooth receivers advertise only work if your phone supports them too. An iPhone sends audio using AAC, so LDAC and aptX Adaptive go unused. That's why we list it explicitly as a weakness of the FiiO BR13 in our overview: its hi-res codecs don't work with an iPhone.

That leads to a simple rule: if you have an iPhone, a receiver with LDAC makes you pay for something you can't use. AirPlay 2 through a streamer is the smarter route. With a compatible Android phone, on the other hand, an inexpensive receiver like the BR13 gets a lot more out of Bluetooth via LDAC or aptX Adaptive than the basic SBC standard does.

The underrated part: your amp's inputs

Streamer or receiver, at some point the data has to become an audio signal again. That's the job of the DAC, the digital-to-analog converter. If your amp only has analog RCA inputs, like most older receivers and amps, the converter inside the add-on box does that job. Then its analog output is what matters.

This is exactly where the WiiM Pro has its weak spot. German site hifi.de rates it 7.8 out of 10 overall, but calls the analog output only adequate in sound quality. Connected via its optical (Toslink) or coaxial output to an amp with a digital input or to an external DAC, it performs noticeably better. If you plan to use the WiiM with an analog-only stereo, factor in the cost of a converter right away. Which DACs have an optical input and an RCA output is shown in our comparison of USB DACs under $200, and whether the extra step is worth it for you is covered in Is an external DAC worth it?.

The Bluetooth receivers we picked also have digital outputs. The BR13 outputs up to 96 kHz and 24-bit over optical and coaxial, which means a higher sample rate (measurements per second) and bit depth (how fine each measurement is) than a CD.

How to decide

  • If you have an iPhone, get a Wi-Fi streamer with AirPlay 2.
  • If you listen to Spotify, Tidal, Qobuz or Amazon Music every day, a streamer is worth it, because the music plays independently of your phone.
  • If you want music in several rooms, there's no way around a streamer.
  • If you have an Android phone with LDAC or aptX Adaptive and only listen occasionally, a Bluetooth receiver is enough.
  • If guests should be able to play their music quickly, Bluetooth is less hassle, because nobody has to join your Wi-Fi.
  • If your stereo should also play from your computer, a receiver with a built-in USB DAC is handy.
  • If your amp only has analog inputs and you go with the WiiM, plan on an external DAC or accept the weaker analog output.
You don't necessarily have to buy anything: many amps have a free AUX input, and a simple cable from your laptop is a decent start for occasional listening.

FAQ

Does the WiiM Pro do Bluetooth too?

Yes, it receives over Bluetooth 5.1, but without high-resolution codecs. That's fine for guests, but Bluetooth isn't meant to be the main way you use the WiiM.

Do I need especially good Wi-Fi for a streamer?

A stable home network where the stereo sits helps. The WiiM Pro also has an Ethernet port; if there's a network cable near your stereo, that's the most reliable connection.

Does Bluetooth with LDAC sound as good as Wi-Fi?

LDAC is the most advanced codec among these devices, but it's still a compressed wireless link between phone and receiver. Over Wi-Fi, that Bluetooth leg disappears. How much of the difference you can hear on your system also depends on the converter, the amp and the speakers.

How far does a Bluetooth receiver reach?

It depends on the device, your walls and your phone. For the FiiO BTA30 Pro with its external antenna and for the Audioengine B1, the sources each quote up to about 30 meters (roughly 100 ft). That's a line-of-sight figure; through walls and ceilings it drops. If you want to control the music from your phone in another room, a streamer on Wi-Fi is more relaxed.

What does each route cost?

A good Bluetooth receiver like the BR13 sits in the budget range, well under $100. The WiiM Pro is closer to $200. If your amp has no digital input, add an external DAC to get the best sound from the WiiM. The BTA30 Pro sits in between and can double as a USB DAC for your computer if you need one.

Can I connect a turntable to the WiiM Pro?

The WiiM Pro has an analog RCA input but no phono preamp. A turntable still needs its own preamp before you plug it in there.

In the end, your daily routine matters more than the tech: the way you listen tells you which device belongs on your stereo.
